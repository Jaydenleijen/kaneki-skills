# Judicial Voice for Client Correspondence

Write client letters, emails and advice that read the way senior Australian judges write: clear, measured, precise, and free of hype and legalese. The point is to **borrow the judges' clarity, tone and vocabulary for correspondence with clients**, not to make client documents sound like court judgments. A client advice is still an advice. A letter is still a letter. What changes is the quality of the prose: the issue named plainly, the answer given early, the reasons ordered, the position calibrated honestly, and every sentence carrying its weight.

The style profile was measured from 1,504 judgments (2024-26) of the High Court and the Queensland, New South Wales and Victorian appeal and supreme courts. Those judgments are the **source of the clarity**, not a template. The rules below are the summary; [references/exemplars.md](references/exemplars.md) shows the writing to learn the clarity from (read it to feel the cadence, then translate it into client prose); [references/vocabulary.md](references/vocabulary.md) is the phrasebank; [references/corpus-stats.md](references/corpus-stats.md) holds the numbers; [references/judges.md](references/judges.md) profiles individual judges you can lean toward.

## What to take from the judges, and what to leave

**Take** (this is the whole point):

- **Clarity of structure.** Name the question, give the answer, then the ordered reasons. No suspense.
- **Measured authority.** State the position in the plain indicative. Confidence comes from the bare statement, never from "clearly", "obviously" or "very".
- **Honest calibration.** Where the position is not certain, say so precisely: "on balance", "the better view is", "it is at least arguable", "we cannot be certain that". Concede what should be conceded, then say why it does not change the answer.
- **Plain, precise vocabulary.** The judicial word-list (relevantly, properly construed, having regard to, in respect of, to the extent that, the better view) minus the courtroom-only terms. No legalese, no AI-isms.
- **Short verdict sentences.** The sentence that carries the conclusion is short and flat. "That is the key risk." "The clause does not help you." "The better course is to wait."
- **One idea per sentence, one step per paragraph.**

**Leave** (these belong to judgments, not client letters):

- Dispositions and orders: "The appeal must be dismissed", "dismissed with costs", "I would allow the appeal". Your equivalent is a **recommendation**: "Our advice is that you should ...".
- Court self-reference: "this Court", "their Honours", "the primary judge erred", "the Court below". You are writing to a client, not about an appeal.
- The verdict formula aimed at a party: "That submission must be rejected." Aimed at a client it is cold. Soften to an **assessment**: "That argument is unlikely to succeed", "We do not think that argument will hold".
- Catchwords, headnotes, medium-neutral citations in the body, paragraph numbers, "REASONS FOR JUDGMENT".
- Deciding the client's case as though from the bench. You advise on likely outcomes and give a recommendation; you do not pronounce.

## Core rules

1. **Lead with the answer, then give the reasons.** Judges name the issue and answer it before reasoning; do the same for the client. Open the substance with the bottom line: "In short, the contract lets you terminate, but only after giving 14 days' notice. The reasons follow." Do not bury the answer under background. If there are several questions, number them and answer each.

2. **Certainty is the plain indicative, not intensifiers.** The corpus almost never uses "clearly" or "obviously" (under 1 per 10,000 words). Write "The clause does not apply here", not "It is very clear that the clause obviously does not apply". Delete "clearly", "obviously", "very", "extremely", "highly", "strongly", "quite", "really", "undoubtedly", "of course". A firm point is made firmly by stating it once.

3. **Calibrate honestly; never overstate to reassure.** This is the most valuable thing to borrow. A judge's confidence is earned. Use the judicial hedges, which are precise, not vague:
   - **Concede then pivot:** "It may be accepted that the email looks like an agreement. But it was never signed, and on balance a court would not treat it as binding." (the single most useful move for advice)
   - "The better view is that ..." / "On balance, ..." / "It is at least arguable that ..."
   - "We are not persuaded that ..." / "We cannot be confident that ..." / "There is a real risk that ..."
   Say what you do not know: "We cannot advise on X until we see the trust deed." Never write "it could be argued", "arguably" as a reflex, or "we would suggest" as filler.

4. **Address the client and name the parties; drop court self-reference.** Write "you", and refer to the other side by name ("the vendor", "Mr Xerri", "the Council"). When you describe what a court would do, say so plainly: "A court would, in our view, read the clause narrowly." Never "this Court", "their Honours", "the primary judge".

5. **First person is for assessment, not feeling.** Use "we" for the firm (or "I" for a single author), and only in the assessment verbs the judges use: "we accept", "we consider", "in our view", "we are not persuaded", "we do not think", "our advice is". Never "we feel", "we believe", "we would argue", "I reckon", "we are confident" (say why instead).

6. **Vary sentence length; let the short sentence land the point.** A long explanatory sentence followed by a short flat one is the rhythm. "The lease is silent on make-good, and the common law will not imply an obligation where the parties have addressed the topic elsewhere in the document. So you are not liable for the fit-out." Do not write three long sentences in a row.

7. **Order the reasons and the asks.** Use "First, ... Second, ... Third," or a colon lead-in with parallel, semicolon-separated items when you need something from the client: "To firm up this position, please send us: the signed contract; any emails with the agent before 2 October; and details of what you spent in reliance on the deal." Noun-led, parallel, no full stop on the last item.

8. **Sentence-initial "But" and "Nor" are good plain English.** "But" opens sentences freely in the corpus (three times as often as "However"). Use it. "Nor" is the natural negative link. Avoid "Furthermore", "Additionally", "Moreover" (rare and dated in the corpus).

9. **State principle as settled, then apply it to the facts.** "The law here is not controversial. A contract is read as a whole, by its text, context and purpose. Applied to your agreement, that means ...". Follow the statement of law with a one-sentence application to the client's situation.

10. **Explain causation with the judicial connectives.** "That is because", "It follows that", "For that reason", "In any event", "Relevantly", "In other words", "That is,". Use "Relevantly" to flag the fact or term that matters. Avoid "therefore" more than once a page and "hence" almost entirely.

11. **Frame the ask around its purpose, then make it.** Do not issue a bare instruction. State why first: "So that we can assess the estoppel argument, please pull together anything showing what you did in reliance on the promise." Purpose before directive.

12. **Define a shorthand once, then use it.** "the Deed of Loan and Guarantee (the Deed)", "St Vincent's Private Hospitals Ltd (the Hospital)". Thereafter use the defined term without variation. Do not alternate synonyms for the same thing.

13. **Use the judicial vocabulary, not legalese.** Favour: relevantly, properly construed, on its proper construction, having regard to, in respect of, in so far as, to the extent that, for the purposes of, the better view, apt, on balance, in any event, save that, albeit, engaged, purported. Sparingly: prima facie, onus, notwithstanding, whilst. Never: hereinafter, aforesaid, the said, hereby, herein, wherein, thereto, forthwith, aforementioned, herewith, thereof (all effectively zero in the corpus). Keep court-only terms out of client letters: "the impugned decision", "constructional choice", "enlivened", "the plurality" read as jargon to a client; prefer "the decision in question", "which reading is correct", "engaged", "the majority".

14. **Australian conventions throughout.** -ise spellings; dates as 17 March 2022, Friday, 14 May; "s 23", "s 68(1)", "cl 4", "Pt 2"; "on foot" for a live agreement; "100% refundable". No em dashes (zero in the High Court corpus; restructure the sentence instead). No exclamation marks. Contractions are fine in a warm client email but keep them light; an advice memo has none.

15. **Close with the recommendation and the next step, not a disposition.** Where a judgment ends "For these reasons, the appeal is dismissed", a client document ends with what the client should do: "Our advice is that you should not sign until the make-good clause is amended. If you would like, we will prepare the amendment and send it to the vendor's solicitor." Clean, specific, and it hands the decision back to the client.

## Translating judicial moves into client correspondence

| Judicial move (in a judgment) | The same clarity, written to a client |
|---|---|
| "The determinative issue is whether X. For the following reasons, the answer is 'no'." | "The real question is whether X. The short answer is no, and here is why." |
| "That submission must be rejected." | "That argument is unlikely to succeed." / "We do not think that argument will hold." |
| "It may be accepted that X. But Y." | "It is true that X. But Y, and on balance that is what matters." |
| "The appeal must be dismissed with costs." | "Our advice is that you should not pursue this. The likely cost outweighs the prospects." |
| "The primary judge erred in finding X." | "The other side is likely to argue X. We think that argument is wrong, because ..." |
| "It is well established that ..." | "The law on this is settled: ..." |
| "For these reasons, I would allow the appeal." | "For those reasons, our advice is that you have a strong position on this point." |
| "It is unnecessary to decide whether X." | "We do not need to resolve X to answer your question." |
| "There is no substance in that contention." | "There is little to that point." |
| "The order is ..." | "The next step is ..." / "What we recommend is ..." |

## Worked example

**Rough draft (what a lawyer might dash off):**

> Hi Rob, thanks for reaching out! Just wanted to circle back on your query. So basically I think you probably have a pretty strong case here and it's very likely you'll be able to get out of the lease, though obviously there are some risks we'd need to navigate. The landlord is clearly in the wrong in my opinion. Happy to jump on a call to discuss! Let me know your thoughts.

**Redrafted in judicial voice, still a client email:**

> Hi Rob.
>
> On your question whether you can end the lease early: the better view is that you can, but not without risk, and there is one thing we need before we can be confident.
>
> The lease has no break clause. That ordinarily means you are bound for the full term. But the landlord's failure to repair the roof, if it is a breach of an essential term, would let you terminate at common law. On the material we have, that failure looks serious enough to qualify.
>
> The risk is proof. It may be accepted that the roof has leaked for months. But you would need to show that you gave the landlord a proper chance to fix it and that they did not. So that we can firm up this position, please send us: the dates you reported the leak; any written responses from the landlord; and photographs, if you have them.
>
> Our advice is that you should not stop paying rent yet. Doing so now would put you in breach and weaken your position. Once we have the material above, we will tell you whether you can safely give a notice to terminate.
>
> Kind regards,

Note what changed: the answer moved to the top; every intensifier ("pretty strong", "very likely", "clearly", "obviously") went; "reach out", "circle back", "jump on a call", "happy to", the exclamation marks all went; the position is calibrated ("the better view", "if it is a breach", "looks serious enough"); the crux is isolated in a short sentence ("The risk is proof."); the ask is framed by its purpose and set out as parallel items; and it closes with a recommendation and a clear next step.

## Anti-patterns

- **Deciding instead of advising:** "You will win." "The clause is void." Advise on likelihood and give a recommendation: "You have a strong position", "the clause is probably unenforceable, and our advice is ...".
- **Judgment furniture in a letter:** "this Court", "the primary judge", "must be rejected", "dismissed with costs", catchwords, paragraph numbers. Wrong register for a client.
- **Overclaiming to reassure:** "clearly", "obviously", "very strong", "no doubt", "definitely", "guaranteed". Calibrate instead.
- **AI-isms:** delve, navigate, landscape, crucial, pivotal, robust, nuanced, holistic, leverage, streamline, showcase, testament to, "in conclusion", "additionally", "furthermore". All at or near zero in 12 million words of judgments.
- **Chatty filler:** "reach out", "circle back", "jump on a call", "touch base", "happy to help", "just", "quick", "great", "thanks for reaching out". A warm greeting and a clean close are enough.
- **Reflex hedging:** "it could be argued", "it may well be", "arguably" as a tic, "we would suggest". Hedge only where the uncertainty is real, and say what it is.
- **Legalese:** hereinafter, aforesaid, the said contract, hereby, herewith, wherein, thereof. Plain words instead.
- **Em dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical-question chains, whole-sentence bolding.**

## How to apply

1. **Find the question the client actually has.** Write it in one sentence. If there are several, number them.
2. **Answer it first.** One or two sentences, up top, before any background. Give the bottom line, calibrated.
3. **Order the reasons.** State the relevant law as settled, then apply it to the client's facts. "First, ... Second, ...". One step per paragraph, ending on a short sentence.
4. **Calibrate every claim.** Pass over each statement and add honest qualification where certainty is not real ("on balance", "the better view", "there is a risk that"). Concede the good point against you, then say why it does not change the advice.
5. **Frame every ask by its purpose,** and set lists as noun-led, semicolon-separated parallel items.
6. **Close with a recommendation and the next step,** not a pronouncement. Hand the decision back to the client.
7. **Audit pass.** Cut every intensifier, every AI-ism, every chatty filler, every em dash, every exclamation mark, every piece of judgment furniture ("this Court", "must be rejected"). Check the answer is in the first paragraph. Check "we"/"I" is followed by an assessment verb. Check the closing sentence is a recommendation, not a decision.
8. **Read it as if across a desk from the client.** Measured, not stiff; confident where confidence is earned, honest where it is not.

## Register and pairing

- **Client email:** the full voice, lighter. A warm one-line greeting ("Hi, Rob."), contractions allowed, close with a recommendation and offer of the next step.
- **Formal advice / letter of advice / memo:** the full voice, no contractions, headed by the question and the short answer, then numbered issues; end with the recommendation.
- **File note:** the same clarity, telegraphic where useful; short verdict sentences throughout.
- **A letter to the other side:** measured and non-emotive is exactly right; keep the calibration but state the client's position firmly, and drop the client-directed softening ("we do not think that will hold" becomes "your client's position is not sustainable, for the following reasons").
- **Pairs with `dan-voice`:** this skill sets the register, clarity and vocabulary; `dan-voice` (Daniel Devitt's personal voice) supplies the greeting, warmth calibration and sign-off for Kaneki Law client comms. When the user asks for Dan's voice, use `dan-voice` and let this skill inform the clarity underneath. For Zac or Cozee comms, use this skill's plain-English register directly.
- **Court flavours** (from [references/judges.md](references/judges.md)) are still useful as dials: Freeburn J and the Queensland trial judges for the plainest, shortest-sentence advice on facts and evidence; the High Court joint reasons and Gordon J for structured advice on a hard point of law; Victoria's "we ... the judge ... single quotes" if the matter is Victorian. But every one of these is a source of clarity to translate, never a template to copy into a client's inbox.
