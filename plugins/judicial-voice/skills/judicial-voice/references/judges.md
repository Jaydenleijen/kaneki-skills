# Individual judges

Use these when the user asks for a particular judge's flavour, or to pick a register for a task. Figures are per 10,000 words of that judge's separately authored reasons in the corpus (joint reasons are attributed to no one). All are current members of the relevant court as at September 2026.

**Gageler CJ** writes mostly in joint reasons (with Gordon and Beech-Jones JJ, or with Gleeson and Jagot JJ). The joint style: the issue stated in the first sentence as a proposition of law; the earlier authority summarised and then qualified ("However, to only so state is to give an incomplete description of the effect of Smith and Afford. Relevantly, three further propositions ... should be noted. The first proposition is that ... The second proposition is that ... The third proposition is that ..."); then "Background"; then analysis; then a one-line disposition. Heavy use of "relevantly", "properly", "the better view". Structured, numbered, no ornament.

**Gordon J.** The most direct of the current bench. Opens with the facts in two sentences, names the authorities, then states the case's importance plainly: "This case raises issues of fundamental importance concerning the separation of judicial power and the rule of law. The rule of law requires that everyone, including the Executive, obey the law." Uses "I" more than most (12.9), "As will be explained", "For the following reasons, the answer is 'no'." Frequent colons introducing the point. Sentence-initial "But" and "Nor". Rarely "accordingly".

**Edelman J.** The essayist. Openings with an anecdote or a striking sentence ("Imprisonment for stupidity" as a heading; "It is said that, after the House of Lords decision in DPP v Smith, Fullagar J walked into Dixon CJ's chambers and said, 'Well, Dixon, they're hanging men for manslaughter in England now'"). Questions as headings ("How far can a court go to save legislation and regulations from invalidity?"). Highest rate of sentence-initial "But" (24.9) and "In other words". Almost never "accordingly" (0.4). Explains by restating: "In other words, it was a miscarriage of justice to allow the jury to think that a finding of one state of mind could be inferred from a finding of a different state of mind." Reaches for history, comparative law, and the occasional vivid image ("No court ... enjoys a licence to abandon the principled development of the law at the altar of ad hoc teleological reasoning under a mask of judicial minimalism"). Use for opinions that need to persuade as well as decide.

**Steward J.** Shortest sentences on the bench (median 24) and by far the most first person (30.1). "My brief reasons are as follows." "I do not think there was a real risk that ..." "In my view, the officer is indeed liable in such circumstances." Poses the dilemma as a direct question and answers it in the next sentence: "Before this Court is a simple dilemma: should an officer of the Commonwealth be liable for acting in accordance with that officer's duty ...? In my view, the officer is indeed liable in such circumstances." Heavy colons (36). Concurs graciously: "I respectfully agree with the answer given by Gordon J ... I also gratefully adopt Gordon J's description of facts and issues." Use for short, decisive advice.

**Gleeson J.** Fewest short sentences (2.7%). Even, measured, cumulative; frequent "accordingly" (4.1). Concurs briefly and precisely. Use when the tone must be unhurried.

**Jagot J.** Longest sentences (median 36), most semicolons, most "accordingly" (5.2). Dense, exhaustive treatment of the statutory scheme; every proposition footnoted. "Put another way, there is no deficiency in the implementation of the German Agreement that is so substantial as to deny s 5(1) the character of a measure implementing that agreement." Use for detailed statutory opinions.

**Beech-Jones J.** Highest "however" (13.7), lowest colons. Careful, qualifying, criminal-law fluent. Resolves crisply when ready: "The issue is readily resolved: plainly it is properly so characterised." Use for balanced treatment of competing considerations.

## Queensland Court of Appeal (2026 bench)

Bowskill CJ, Mullins P, Bond JA, Boddice JA, Brown JA, Bradley JA, Doyle JA, with Burns SJA, Martin SJA and trial judges sitting as required. Court of Appeal reasons are shorter-sentenced than the High Court (median 22 words), use "I" freely in single-author reasons (29 per 10,000), and favour "in my view" (3.0), "the learned primary judge", "with respect", "there is no substance in this ground", "no error is shown". Joint reasons are signed "THE COURT:".

**Bond JA.** Analytical, numbered, procedural. Sets out grounds "in full", then: "In substance (and not necessarily by reference to the grounds set out in the Notice of Appeal) the appellant contended ...". Disposes of hopeless points shortly: "They have failed." Contract construction is his register: "The relevant principles of construction are not controversial. The agreement is to be construed objectively, by reference to its text, context and purpose."

**Mullins P.** Plain narrative, particulars laid out one per sentence, concise concurrence: "I am not persuaded that further information makes any difference to the outcome of this appeal. I agree with Justice Burns that the appellant has not demonstrated any appellable error."

**Brown JA.** Procedural and applications work; "The Registrar was entitled under r 982 to do so." Direct, short, uses "which I will refer to as".

**Boddice JA.** Criminal appeals; "On 7 November 2024, a jury found the appellant guilty of ..." Short factual openings, then grounds, then "There is no substance in this ground."

**Doyle JA.** Planning and statutory appeals; states the leave test in the first paragraph, then the point.

## Queensland Supreme Court trial division (most prolific in corpus)

Freeburn J (24 judgments), Hindman J (19), McCafferty J (17), Kelly J (17), Cooper J (15), Smith J (14), Treston J (13), Williams J (11), Ryan J (11), Henry J, Davis J, Copley J, Wilson J, Muir J, Martin SJA, Crow J, Sullivan J, Burns SJA, Bowskill CJ.

**Freeburn J.** The clearest model for plain judicial English. Short declaratives for facts: "The employment did not go well." "That did not work." "The end result is that some caution is needed." "It is necessary to consider each of the contested paragraphs." Sentence-initial "And so," and "And,". States principle then applies: "The privilege, of course, is a substantive right. And, in assessing the risk of self-incrimination, it is necessary to appreciate that ..." Frequent "It is relevant to note two further considerations. The first is ... The second is ...". Use as the default for advice letters in judicial voice.

**Hindman J.** Methodical, checklist reasoning against numbered requirements ("[requirement (b)] can be presumed because ..."), explicit inference language: "that would be a reasonable inference to draw", "I consider that presumption ... rebutted in circumstances where ...".

**Kelly J.** Procedural law and costs; traces the history of a rule through the authorities, then: "I reject that submission." Uses "Notably," "relevantly said", "The 'something' was not articulated."

**Bowskill CJ.** States "The key issue for determination is whether ..." in the first paragraphs; lays out each side's submission in one paragraph each; identifies the onus; then decides. Uses "Whilst", "(as her Honour then was)".

## Choosing a register

- Point-of-law opinion for a sophisticated reader: Gageler CJ joint style or Gordon J.
- Opinion that must persuade a sceptic: Edelman J.
- Short decisive advice: Steward J.
- Advice on facts, evidence and procedure: Freeburn J or Bond JA.
- Balanced weighing of competing considerations: Beech-Jones J.
- Costs, procedure, rules: Kelly J.

## New South Wales Court of Appeal (2025-26 bench)

Lead authorship in the corpus runs Bell CJ (Chief Justice) and Ward P (President) most often, then Leeming, Mitchelmore, Payne, Kirk, Adamson, Free, Stern and Gleeson JJA, with Basten AJA and others sitting. Court of Appeal reasons run a little shorter than the High Court (median sentence 28 words) and use "I" freely in single-author reasons (30 per 10,000). The register: name the ground, state the principle, apply it, dispose. Stock phrases: "the primary judge", "his Honour"/"her Honour", "with respect", "no error is shown", "the submission is not to the point", "must be rejected", "I would allow/dismiss the appeal", "leave to appeal must be refused with costs".

**Bell CJ.** The plainest opener: one or two sentences locating the appeal and the judgment below, then the issue. "This is an appeal from a decision of Pike J (the primary judge) sitting in the Equity Division of this Court." Measured, structural, economical.

**Leeming JA.** Doctrinal and precise; frames the question as "narrow and important", carries the analysis through defined terms, disposes without flourish. Use for questions of law and construction.

**Ward P.** Thorough on the facts, careful with the procedural history, explicit about what is and is not in dispute.

**Adamson JA, Payne JA, Mitchelmore JA, Kirk JA.** The working appellate voice: grounds taken in turn, each opened "Ground N", principle stated, then "That ground should be rejected" or "is not made out". Kirk JA leans to statutory-scheme analysis; Adamson JA to crisp findings.

**Basten AJA.** Concurs tightly and says why in a sentence, adding only a point of difference: "I do not depart from anything Adamson JA has written."

## Victorian Court of Appeal (2026 bench)

Members appearing as lead author in the corpus include Niall CJ, Emerton P, and Beach, Kenny, Whelan, Taylor, Kaye, Kidd, Richards, Orr, T Forrest and Walker JJA, with Donaghue, John Dixon, Jane Dixon, Elliott, Nichols, Fox and Tsalamandris AJJA, and McCann JR (Judicial Registrar) on procedural referrals.

The Victorian voice has three tells that set it apart from the other courts:

1. **"We", not "I".** A two- or three-judge bench writes in the first person plural throughout: "We do not agree", "In our view", "we have concluded", "We would therefore grant leave". Reserve "I" for a single-judge application.
2. **"The judge", not "the primary judge".** Victoria calls the judge below "the judge" (occasionally "the sentencing judge", "the trial judge").
3. **Single quotation marks**, and free use of the dash. Double quotes are almost absent.

Its criminal-appeal openings are formulaic and worth copying: the date and court of the plea or verdict, the charges listed in full, the sentence set out, then the single ground. "The applicant now seeks leave to appeal on the following single ground". Recurring vocabulary: "leave to appeal", "manifestly excessive", "instinctive synthesis", "totality", "the total effective sentence", "without merit", "totally without merit", "it would be futile", "there is no substance in the contention".

The reasoning moves are the same as the other courts: "It may be accepted that X. But Y."; "must be rejected"; "the judge was correct to"; "For these reasons ... we would ...". Use the Victorian profile when drafting sentencing or criminal-appeal advice, or anything for a Victorian court; use "we" only if the advice genuinely speaks for more than one author, otherwise keep the single-judge "I".
