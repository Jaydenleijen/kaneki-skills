# Exemplars

Real passages from judgments, grouped by the rhetorical move they perform. **These are the source of the clarity to borrow into client correspondence, not templates to copy.** Read the group that matches what you are about to write, feel the cadence, connective choice and where the short sentence lands, then translate it into client prose (see the translation table and worked example in SKILL.md). A client letter never contains "this Court", "the appeal must be dismissed" or "the primary judge"; it borrows the structure, the calibration and the plain vocabulary underneath those passages. Each is quoted from the published reasons and attributed.

## 1. Opening: issue first, answer second

> The Commonwealth accepted that if its officers do not have a defence, then the Commonwealth also does not have a defence and it will be vicariously liable for the unlawful detention by the officers. As a result, the determinative issue is whether officers of the Commonwealth have a defence. For the following reasons, the answer is "no".
> This case raises issues of fundamental importance concerning the separation of judicial power and the rule of law. The rule of law requires that everyone, including the Executive, obey the law and that, where the Executive exceeds its powers, there must be effective legal remedies.
> (Gordon J, Abdel-Hady v Commonwealth [2026] HCA 17)

> Before this Court is a simple dilemma: should an officer of the Commonwealth be liable for acting in accordance with that officer's duty, as accurately and lawfully perceived at that time, where by reason of a subsequent decision of this Court the officer is later found to have thereby acted unlawfully? In my view, the officer is indeed liable in such circumstances.
> (Steward J, Abdel-Hady v Commonwealth [2026] HCA 17)

> The issues in this proceeding have resolved. The only issue that remains is the costs of the proceeding. Both parties have filed written submissions. Both seek their costs. The costs issue is to be decided 'on the papers'. It is necessary to examine the factual background and the course of the proceeding because there has been no decision on the merits.
> (Freeburn J, Bundaberg Friendly Society Medical Institute Ltd v Wilson [2026] QSC 1)

> The key issue for determination is whether the replacement contract that was entered into, after the cancelled agreement, between the same seller and a new buyer ... was a resale agreement under ss. 115(1)(d) and (2) of the DA.
> (Bowskill CJ, [2025] QSC 105)

> The principal issue in this appeal is, as Leeming JA said in the judgment under appeal, both "narrow and important": where a successor trustee knows of a former trustee's equitable proprietary interest in the trust estate ... is the obligation ... a fiduciary obligation?
> ([2025] HCA 1)

## 13. A whole paragraph to model (High Court, joint)

> The fault element for the physical element in s 307.1(1)(a) is intention; that is, the person meant to import the substance. The fault element for the physical element in s 307.1(1)(b) is recklessness; that is, the person was reckless with respect to the substance imported being a border controlled drug. Absolute liability attaches to the physical element in s 307.1(1)(c), being that the quantity of the substance imported is a commercial quantity. Further, if on the same occasion a person imports different parcels of border controlled drugs, then the person may be charged with a single offence in respect of all or any of the different parcels of drugs under s 307.1(1). As there were no border controlled drugs secreted within the dough mixer when it came into Australia, the respondent was charged with attempting to commit an offence under s 307.1(1). The physical elements for an attempt to commit an offence under s 307.1(1) are the same as the substantive offence save that the conduct of the accused must only involve an attempt, although the accused's conduct "must be more than merely preparatory to the commission of the offence". The fault elements for attempting to commit an offence under s 307.1(1) are the same as for the substantive offence. Accordingly, the elements of the offence ... that the prosecution had to prove beyond reasonable doubt against the respondent were: (1) ...; (2) ...
> (Gageler CJ, Gordon and Beech-Jones JJ, The King v Ko [2026] HCA 29)

Note the mechanics: parallel sentences with identical spine ("The fault element for ... is ...; that is, ..."), "Further," to add, "As ... , ..." for causation, "save that" for the exception, "Accordingly," to land the enumerated conclusion.

## 14. A whole paragraph to model (Queensland trial judge)

> A successful prosecution will necessarily involve proof of what might be described as building blocks as well as the central allegations. And so, a requirement to positively plead may mean that Mr Ebert's defence could be used directly in possible proceedings, or it could be used as a foundation or to complement other evidence, or it may be used in further inquiries to discover incriminating evidence.
> (Freeburn J, [2025] QSC 109)

Note: "necessarily", "what might be described as", "And so," to open the application, three alternatives joined by "or ... or ... or" in a rising series.

## 15. New South Wales Court of Appeal

The NSW Court of Appeal writes in the same register: issue named, reasons ordered, disposition flat. First person appears in single-author reasons ("I would allow", "in my opinion"); "the primary judge" and "his Honour"/"her Honour" are constant; "with respect" carries disagreement.

Opening, issue first:

> A property developer, IDP 971 Richmond Pty Ltd (the Appellant), entered into a deed on 10 October 2018 (the October Deed) that granted it an option to enter into a contract in a form annexed to the Deed (the Contract) to purchase a property in Marsden Park (the Property) from Mr Jerry Xerri and the late Ms Emanuela Xerri (the Respondents). An earlier option deed (the August Deed) had been terminated on 2 October 2018.
> (Bell CJ, IDP 971 Richmond Pty Ltd v Xerri [2026] NSWCA)

A wry observation, then the point:

> Despite these relatively clear statutory objectives, parties persist in exploring every conceivable argument with a view to encouraging courts to intervene in the operation of the statutory scheme. These proceedings are a further example of this creative persistence, which may reflect the complex nature of some of the statutory provisions and the practical difficulties in applying those provisions to the seemingly infinite factual circumstances underpinning building disputes.
> ([2026] NSWCA)

Rejecting a submission:

> The State also submitted that nine students boarded the bus that T2 was planning to catch and it was not open to a teacher to tell them to disembark and board another bus. The submission is not to the point. It was not those students who T2 had cause to fear and it was not them who caused him harm.

## 16. Victorian Court of Appeal

The Victorian Court of Appeal is distinct in three habits: it writes "we" for a two- or three-judge bench (not "I"), it calls the judge below "the judge" (not "the primary judge"), and it uses single quotation marks. Its criminal work opens with the plea and sentence in plain declaratives; "leave to appeal" and "manifestly excessive" are its recurring frame. "It may be accepted that X. But Y." is used exactly as in the High Court.

Opening a criminal application:

> On 20 May 2025 the applicant pleaded guilty in the County Court to one charge each of making a threat to kill, common law assault, negligently causing serious injury, failing to render assistance after a motor vehicle accident and contravening a family violence intervention order intending to cause harm or fear for safety. ... The victim in respect of each charge was the applicant's then-partner, BG.
> ([2026] VSCA 207)

Rejecting a submission, flatly:

> This argument was fanciful and must be rejected. To begin with, no advocacy by Ms Wellington, or any debate at all, is recorded in the briefing minutes. More significantly, the argument assumes that the other councillors did not have minds of their own but were willing to be led blindly by Ms Wellington. That assumption was without foundation.
> ([2026] VSCA 13)

> There is just no substance to the complaint that the applicant has unfairly been taken by surprise. If he was minded to challenge any use by the judge of the impugned 'I know you know' comment, the applicant had every opportunity to do so.
> ([2026] VSCA 123)

> The applicant's contention that a charge of negligently causing serious injury 'cannot truly be regarded as being an example of family violence' must be rejected. As this Court observed in Skeates the gravity of an offence which has occurred in the context of family violence cannot be properly assessed without considering the surrounding behaviours of the offender.
> ([2026] VSCA 207)

Accepting, and correcting the judge in the same breath:

> The judge was correct to find exceptional circumstances had been established by the appellant. But, in refusing to grant bail on the basis that the prosecution had discharged its onus of establishing unacceptable risk, the judge made a material error. In lieu, in exercising our own discretion we have decided that bail must be granted. Accordingly, the appeal will be allowed and bail will be granted subject to strict conditions.
> ([2026] VSCA 113)

Stating principle:

> It is well established that an extension of time will not be granted if the proposed appeal is 'so devoid of merit that it would be futile to do so'. It is important that this way of framing the relevance of the merits to an application for an extension of time not be misunderstood.
> ([2026] VSCA 213)

Concede then pivot, in the plural voice:

> We do not agree. It may be accepted that the opportunity for recognition was, on one view, limited. In our view, however, it was more substantial than the applicant suggests.
> ([2026] VSCA 15)

Disposition:

> For these reasons, in our view, the contractual text, viewed in context and in light of the purpose of the parties we have identified, points to the correctness of Castleway's construction. We would therefore grant leave and uphold the first ground of appeal.
> ([2026] VSCA 16)
